# Proof that $B_0$ is Not a $\sigma$-Algebra

## **Definitions**
Let $J$ be the collection of all intervals in $[0,1]$, including open, closed, and half-open intervals:

$$
J = \lbrace [a,b], (a,b], [a,b), (a,b) \mid 0 \leq a \leq b \leq 1 \rbrace.
$$

Define $B_0$ as the set of all **finite unions of elements of $J$**:

$$
B_0 = \lbrace \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} I_i \mid I_i \in J \rbrace.
$$

Prove that $B_0$ is **not a $\sigma$-algebra**.

---

## **Proof (by Contradiction)**

1. **Assume that $B_0$ is a $\sigma$-algebra.**
2. Consider the set of rational numbers in $[0,1]$, denoted by $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$
3. $\forall q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1], {q} \in B_0$
4. Since $B_0$ is assumed to be a $\sigma$-algebra, it must be closed under countable unions. Hence, the countable union:

$$
   \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]} {q} = \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]
$$

   must be in $B_0$.

---

### **Case 1: All $I_i$ are singleton intervals**  
   - If every $I_i = [q,q]$, where $q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$, then we have a **countable infinite union of singletons**.
   - But $B_0$ **only allows finite unions**, so we have a contradiction

---

### **Case 2: Suppose we try to write $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$ as a **finite union** of intervals from $J$.**
   - At least one $I_i$ is a proper interval $[a, b]$ with $a < b$
   - $\exists [a, b], a \ne b$ **must contain at least one irrational number**, contradicting the fact that $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$ contains only rationals.

     - The set of irrational numbers is dense in $\mathbb{R}$.
     - For any two rational numbers $a, b$ where $a < b$, there **must exist at least one irrational number in $(a, b)$**.
     - For example, the number:
       $x = a + \frac{(b-a)}{\sqrt{2}}$
       is irrational and lies in $(a,b)$.

   - Since every nontrivial interval $[a,b]$ contains an irrational number, this contradicts the assumption that $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$ can be formed from a **finite union of intervals**.


$$
\Rightarrow B_0 \text{ is NOT a } \sigma \text{-algebra}.
$$
